Build Guide

CURV LAB's official assembly guide (marketed as 67 pages of core content — the full PDF, print-settings appendix included, runs a bit longer), reorganized into 16 sessions of roughly 30 minutes each, sized for a 9-year-old with a grown-up's help — and re-sequenced around an insert press instead of the soldering iron the book calls for. Every insert-pressing step across the whole build now happens before the fastening that follows it, not mixed in partway through. Session 16 (FPV) is optional; everything else gets you a driveable car.

All of the design work here belongs to CURV LAB — the chassis design, the 3D print files, the official Chassis Build Guide WR1 (Version 2), and the reference diagrams below are their work, not ours. This page is just our own re-sequencing of their instructions for how we're building it. If you're building a WR1 yourself, buy the guide and print files straight from them.

🧠 Multiple choice

Why does PETG soak up water from the air in the first place?

PETG is hygroscopic, meaning it chemically attracts and holds onto water vapor right out of the open air — no rain or spills needed, just time sitting on a shelf. That absorbed moisture turns to steam the instant it hits the hot nozzle, which is what causes the weak, bubbly, snap-prone prints drying fixes.

Session 1 — Press the Chassis Plate Inserts

⏱ ~30 min 👤 Kid-led, adult supervises the press 🛠 Insert press (not the soldering iron)
What we changed: the official guide presses these same 10 inserts in two separate moments — step 1.01 (into the two Chassis Plate Posts) and step 1.2 (into the main plate, several pages later in the flow). Since we're using a press instead of a soldering iron, there's no reason to split them — we do all 10 in one sitting, before any fastening starts.

What you need

Chassis Plate
qty 1 · printed, Set A
Chassis Plate Post
qty 2 · printed, Set A
M3 × 5.7mm threaded insert
qty 10 · brass
Insert press + M3 tip
your tool, not the book's

Steps

1

Find your parts: the Chassis Plate (the big long one), the 2 Chassis Plate Posts (small tubes with holes through the middle), and count out 10 brass inserts. Lay them out so nothing rolls away.

2

A grown-up sets up and runs the insert press with the M3 tip — this is the adult's part of the job, every time it comes up. The tip runs at 250–350°F, hot enough to burn on contact, and the press mechanism can pinch fingers too. Hands stay clear while it's running, and clear of a just-pressed insert for a few seconds after — it's still hot.

3

Press 1 insert into each Chassis Plate Post — 2 posts, 2 inserts. Keep the insert straight as it goes in, not tilted.

4

Press the remaining 8 inserts into the main Chassis Plate, at the holes marked in the guide's diagram below.

5

Check every insert: it should sit flush and straight, flat with the plastic, not leaning to one side. If one looks crooked, tell a grown-up — it's easier to back it out and redo it now than after the plate is full of other parts.

6

Put the finished Chassis Plate and both posts into a labeled bag — "Session 2: steering levers & dog bone" — so they're easy to find next time.

Why we use a press instead of a handheld iron: the book's method has you hold a 250–350°F soldering iron by hand and melt each insert in freehand — hot, slow, and easy to touch something you shouldn't. A press heats to that same temperature, but the tip is fixed in a guided vertical mount instead of held loose in a hand, so there's a lot less chance of an accidental touch. It is still a hot tool, not a cold one — that's why running the press stays the adult's job every time, in every session that uses one. Anywhere the guide calls for the soldering iron and insert tips, use the press instead.
🧠 Quick check

Why press a metal insert into the plastic instead of just screwing straight into it?

Show answer

Plastic threads wear out fast — take a screw in and out a few times and the plastic strips, and the screw just spins. A brass insert gives the screw permanent metal threads to bite into instead, so the joint stays tight no matter how many times you take it apart.

🧠 True or false

It doesn't matter which way the Steering Washer's flange side faces when you install it.

False — the guide is specific that the flange side faces down. Getting it backwards usually still "fits," which is exactly what makes this mistake easy to miss until the steering feels wrong later.

🧠 Quick check

When the car turns a corner, does the outside wheel or the inside wheel have to spin faster?

Show answer

The outside wheel — it's tracing a bigger circle, so it has to cover more distance in the same amount of time as the inside wheel. That's the whole reason the differential you just built exists: it lets the two wheels on an axle spin at different speeds instead of forcing them to match.

🧠 Multiple choice

This session uses both glue AND threadlocker on different joints. What's the actual difference?

Glue (like the plastic cement bonding the two bracket halves here) is meant to be permanent — those two parts are one part now. Threadlocker is a thin coating on screw threads that resists vibration, but a screwdriver still backs the screw straight out whenever you need it to.

🧠 Quick check

This motor has three wires, not two — plugging in power alone won't make it spin at all. So how does the ESC actually make it turn, speed up, and reverse?

Show answer

This is a brushless motor, so there's no simple "plug in power and it spins" like a toy motor has. The ESC has to constantly tell the motor's three wires which one gets power next, in order, thousands of times a second — that ongoing sequence is what actually turns the shaft. Speed comes from how much power each step in that sequence carries (rapid on/off switching called PWM, "pulse width modulation") — more power per pulse means more speed. Reverse just means running that same three-wire sequence backward instead of forward.

🧠 True or false

False — a servo only turns to a specific position and holds it, usually across less than one full rotation. That's exactly what steering needs: not "spin forever," but "point exactly this many degrees left or right and stay there."

🧠 Quick check

Why does each wheel get two control arms — one upper, one lower — instead of just one?

Show answer

One arm alone would let the wheel tilt and wobble as it moved up and down over a bump. Two parallel arms, one above and one below, form a shape called a four-bar linkage — it holds the knuckle at (roughly) the same angle throughout its whole travel, so the wheel stays planted instead of leaning over.

🧠 Multiple choice

You've pressed bearings into nearly every knuckle and diff case in this build. What's a bearing actually doing?

Without a bearing, a spinning axle would just be metal rubbing directly against plastic — slow, hot, and it would wear the hole out fast. A bearing has its own tiny rolling balls inside, so the axle rolls smoothly instead of scraping.

🧠 Quick check

When you turn the wheel, do both front wheels turn by the exact same angle?

Show answer

No — the inside wheel (the one closer to the turn) actually swings a little sharper than the outside wheel, because it's tracing a tighter circle. Steering links are positioned so this happens automatically; it's called Ackermann geometry, named after the guy who worked it out.

🧠 Quick check

Each shock has a metal spring AND an oil-filled cylinder. Why does it need both?

Show answer

The spring is what soaks up the bump and pushes the wheel back down — on its own, it would keep bouncing like a pogo stick. The oil-filled cylinder (the "damper") slows that bounce-back down so the car settles quickly instead of bouncing forever.

Session 15 — ESC, Battery & Receiver

⏱ ~30 min 👤 Kid solders with adult guiding her hands; multimeter check and battery connection are the adult's alone 🛠 Screwdriver + double-stick foam tape + soldering iron + multimeter
No inserts here — this session is tape, straps, and 2 screws. This is the wiring session: everything electrical goes in.
⚠ Field modification — not in the official guide. Kid solders, with very hands-on adult supervision. The ESC ships with a stock XT60 connector, but the Zeee battery we ordered (see shopping list) is EC5-terminated — XT60-terminated batteries are hard to get shipped to the US right now, so we're converting the ESC's connector to EC5 instead of chasing the "correct" one. This is the first time soldering shows up in the build — a real new skill, worth letting her hold the iron — but a hot tip and battery polarity mean an adult stays right there guiding her hands the whole time, not just watching from across the room. The final polarity check with a multimeter, done before Step E, is the adult's alone — no official CURV LAB diagram covers any of this, so the two images here are our own reference drawings, not guide pages.

Converting the ESC connector (do this first, on the bench)

EC5 Connector Set (Amass)
1 pair · the "DEV" male-pin half
Soldering iron — Fanttik T1 Max
Already own. Hobbywing's manual calls for 50W+ on ESC leads; the T1 Max tops out at 16W wired — go slow, keep the tip on each joint longer, and confirm the solder has fully wetted and gone shiny before moving on.
Heat shrink tubing
2 pieces, sized for the ESC leads
Wire strippers
—
Multimeter
to verify polarity before first connection
A

With the ESC unplugged from everything, cut the stock XT60 connector off the ESC's battery leads, leaving as much wire length as you can.

B

Strip about 1/4" (6mm) of insulation from each of the two wires, then tin the exposed strands with a little solder — first time with the iron, so an adult guides your hand on this one.

C

Slide a piece of heat-shrink tubing onto each wire before soldering — easy to forget, hard to add after the joint is made.

D

Solder the red wire into the pin marked + and the black wire into the pin marked − on the EC5 "DEV" connector (the male-pin half) from the Amass set — match the markings printed on the plastic housing, don't guess. Same as Step B: adult hands guide yours on the iron.

E

Shrink the tubing over each joint, then — this part's the adult's alone — check polarity with a multimeter against the battery's own EC5 plug before ever connecting a battery. Reversed polarity destroys the ESC and isn't covered under warranty; this check is what actually catches a soldering mistake before it costs you an ESC.
